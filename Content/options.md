# Part 4 — Marketing points that may deserve a section of their own

Candidates. Each is currently either buried in a sentence or absent entirely.

### P1 — The second use case, as its own section

*"It's a placement tool, but it's also a marketing tool."* Right now territory work is spread across four sections in the long decks and folded into a closing line in the short ones. It is half the product and the half an owner is actually buying.
**Argues for:** its own section, adjacent to placement, explicitly named as the second job.

### P2 — Their knowledge compounds

Forty homes ruled out before anyone was contacted, because their team had already ruled them out. Every deck mentions blacklisting as a feature bullet. None of them notice that it means the system gets better at their territory the longer they use it — which is a retention argument and a differentiation argument at once.
**Argues for:** a small section, or the strongest line inside the concierge section.

### P3 — The pre-tour is a marketing visit

Her slide 5: *"Every Pre-Tour Becomes a Marketing Opportunity."* Not in any of our nine decks. It is the sentence that stops placement and territory being two disconnected products — the client tour and the relationship visit are the same visit.
**Argues for:** either its own section, or the join that removes the need for one.

### P4 — Discovered, not designed

*"A use case that I didn't even think of at all… what John had told me is that hospice reps are going to homes to try to get their business."* A product that grew a second half because a customer said something. Credible, human, and unavailable to any competitor.
**Argues for:** a short section, or a line in an About page rather than the homepage.

### P5 — Why the homes matter, with a person in it

*"I'm a home owner. I have a dude that's dying and I need a hospice."* Our version — *"homes decide which hospice gets called when a resident starts declining"* — is the same fact with the person removed.
**Argues for:** rewriting the existing coverage section rather than adding one.

### P6 — Oversight and reporting

Two paid add-ons, one real reason (*"they're at home acting like they're working"*), and only one deck puts it on the page at all.
**Argues for:** probably not a homepage section. Possibly one line. Your call.

### P7 — The family route

The only part of the product that touches a family. Gets its own section in comparison B and DS Extended, one line elsewhere, and the positioning document still has an open question about whether it is public at all.
**Argues for:** resolving the open question before deciding.

### P8 — The proof we already have

The message from a home in her slide 6 — *"…We do our own placements, saving you from paying any placement agent fees… Can't wait to meet!"* Three decks hold an empty RESERVED slot for a customer quote. This is not that, and it may be better than that: it is the market reacting to the pitch.
**Argues for:** filling the reserved slot with the thing that exists.

### P9 — The discharge clock

Comparison Extended only. *"A patient has a discharge date. The coordinator working the phones against it loses days."* The one urgency argument in the entire body of material.
**Argues for:** a section if we sell to coordinators, a line if we sell to owners.

# Part 5 — Constraints that narrow the options above

Added after reading the client message thread (`extract/11-client-brief-messages.md`). These are not preferences. They eliminate options.

**The page gets forwarded up a ladder.** There is a company with 10 users ready to sign; the champion knows her, their leadership does not. The site has to hold up cold, on a phone, in front of someone who will never meet her.

- **Kills H4** — *"Somebody is walking into those homes. It isn't your team."* The person forwarding this upward is very likely the liaison manager. A hook that indicts liaisons is handed to the reader by a liaison.
- **Weakens H2** — its second sentence narrates our work, and does it in the first thing a stranger reads.
- **Strengthens H1, H3, H9** — all three work with no introduction and no prior relationship.
- **Raises the bar on the close.** "Bring us a territory" assumes a warm reader. An executive three steps removed needs the offer stated plainly.

**Looking homemade is the failure she names, not saying the wrong thing.** *"I don't want it to look like I made it myself in 2005."* Design credibility outranks copy cleverness wherever the two compete.

**The video is not a structural slot.** *"Not that it would just be a video on the website or anything."* Comparisons A and C and DS Extended all reserve large video real estate; comparison A specs six minutes. That should shrink or go.

**Imagery budget is 18 iStock credits.** Photography cannot carry multiple sections. The map, the product screens and the branded icons have to do the work — which is the right answer anyway for a company whose brief is *"eldercare, but mostly software and data."*

**She resists the data being the only edge.** Om proposed building around the state record; she agreed it is an edge, *"one of which is your data for sure,"* and pushed back on it being the single one. So the record is a pillar, not the spine.

**The no-recipe rule is hers.** *"IDK what to give away the secret sauce."* She raised it before any of our decks existed. Every ✗ row above is breaking a rule the client set herself — and the sentence behind the rule is that Delpat runs the monthly scrape, which is exactly the ingredient that never gets listed.

# Part 7 — The route walkthrough, as an animation

Missing from this document until now. Four approaches, with what each costs.

### W1 — Scroll-driven spine

The nine branded icons sit in a row. Scrolling advances the active step: icon lights, copy swaps on the left, screen swaps on the right. Between the pre-tour and family-tour steps the map draws its route between stops.
**Costs:** careful scroll-jacking, which is the most common way this goes tacky. **Gains:** the whole flow is one continuous gesture, which is exactly the coherence the page lacks.

### W2 — Reader-driven stepper

Same layout, but the reader clicks. DS Website B already prototyped this with four tabs.
**Costs:** most readers will click twice and leave. **Gains:** nothing moves unless they want it to; passes accessibility with almost no work; a forwarded link opens on a static, complete-looking section.

### W3 — One continuous map journey

No step list. The map is the whole section: it zooms from the metro to the East Valley to a neighbourhood to one home's record, then the pins reduce to five, then the five become a driven route. Copy floats over it, one line per beat.
**Costs:** the most production, and it needs real map frames. **Gains:** by far the most memorable, and comparison Extended already storyboarded the zoom.

### W4 — Static, numbered, no motion

Eight frames down the page.
**Costs:** it is the thing you said did not feel like "the one." **Gains:** it is also the `prefers-reduced-motion` fallback that any of the above needs anyway, so it gets built regardless.

**Worth noting:** W3 and W1 are not exclusive. The map journey could *be* steps 3–7 of the spine.

**The hard line to set, whichever wins:** one narrative animation on the page. Everything else is reveal-on-scroll — fade plus a small rise, on the design system's existing `--dur-reveal` (420ms). No parallax, no counting numbers, no autoplay carousel. The design system already zeroes every duration under `prefers-reduced-motion`, so honouring it costs nothing.

# Part 9 — Sentences that do not sound like a machine wrote them

For calibration, not for use as-is. The benchmark is already in the material: *"A long form never gets filled in a car park."* It works because it is specific, slightly wry, and could only be written by someone who has watched it happen.

More in that register, drawn from the raw sources:

- It re-optimises if you stop for coffee.
- The form asks whether it smelled right. Reps answer that one.
- Somebody at that home is choosing a hospice this month, and it will not be the one that never came.
- The nine homes on that street have one owner and one phone number.
- Your team already knows which homes are a waste of a morning. The list remembers.
- Nobody drives to a home to find out it has no room.

**Patterns to keep off the page,** counted in the current decks: the two-clause full-stop heading (5 occurrences in DS Extended alone), *at a glance* (4 across the material), *purpose-built*, *streamlined*, *seamless*, *comprehensive*, and every section heading sharing one grammatical shape.
